When the founder can't be the whole commercial team

Founder-led selling is how most services firms grow. Past a certain size, it is also what stops them. Six signs you've reached that point, and how to make the transition without losing your best clients.

MECA Advisory · November 2026 · 5 min read

Este artículo está disponible en inglés por ahora.

Look at the founder's calendar in a typical 80-person software services firm. A pitch on Monday. Two pricing approvals on Tuesday. A call to calm down an unhappy client on Wednesday. A renewal dinner on Thursday. Strategy, hiring and product sit in the gaps between.

None of this is a mistake. In services, clients buy trust, and in the early years the founder is the trust. They know every key client, they price on instinct, and they fix problems before they become losses. That is why the company grew.

The problem is that it doesn't scale. At some point, the founder stops being the company's biggest commercial asset and becomes its biggest bottleneck.

Six signs, and a quick test for each

SignQuick test
Every large deal needs the founderDid any deal above your average size close last quarter without them in the room?
Key clients belong to the founder, not the companyIf the founder were away for three months, which top-10 accounts would you be worried about?
Pricing is a negotiation, not a policyCan anyone tell you, today, the margin of your five largest accounts?
Commercial people execute, but don't grow accountsHow much of last year's growth came from an account manager's own initiative?
The forecast is a feelingHow far off was last quarter's forecast, and when did you find out?
The founder has no time for the companyWhen did the founder last spend a full day on strategy, not on clients?

If three or more answers make you uncomfortable, the issue isn't effort. It is structure.

What founder dependency really costs

It rarely shows up as a crisis. It shows up as slow leaks:

  • Concentration risk. Your largest accounts depend on one relationship. When a client sponsor changes, nobody else has the standing to hold the account.
  • Margin leakage. Discounts given to close a deal quickly, rates that were never raised, scope that grew without a change order.
  • A ceiling on growth. The company can only sell as much as one person can personally oversee.
  • A lower company value. Buyers and investors look hard at founder dependency. A business whose revenue walks out the door with one person is worth less.

The founder's job changes, it doesn't disappear

The goal is not to take the founder out of sales. It is to move them from closer to sponsor: present in the most strategic relationships, setting the direction, and backed by a commercial system that works without them in every meeting.

That system has four parts: a named owner for every key account, pricing rules that don't need the founder's approval, a real pipeline and forecast, and a team that is measured on growing accounts, not just serving them.

Why not just hire a head of sales?

Sometimes that's the right answer. But at this stage, a full-time hire often struggles: the search takes months, the cost is high for a firm this size, and the new executive arrives into a function with no process, no data, and a founder who isn't ready to hand over the relationships.

The first job isn't to sell more. It is to build the system someone can then run.

A fractional path in 90 days

A fractional commercial leader — senior, part-time, for a defined period — is often the faster and lower-risk way to build it:

  1. Days 1–30: see the business clearly. Map the client portfolio: which accounts carry the revenue, which are at risk, where the growth is, and what each one really earns.
  2. Days 31–60: set the rules. Put pricing guidelines and approval limits in place, and assign a named owner to every key account.
  3. Days 61–90: transfer and run. Move relationships gradually, with the founder introducing the new owner rather than disappearing. Start a monthly rhythm of pipeline, forecast and account health.

By day 90, the founder has time back, the team owns growth, and it is clear what kind of full-time leader the company needs — and whether it needs one yet.
